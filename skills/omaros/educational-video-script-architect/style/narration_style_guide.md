# Narration Style Guide

The voice-over is the spine of the video. It carries the conceptual logic; the visuals carry the conceptual evidence. Treat narration as something a thoughtful person would actually say to a colleague — not as a teacher reciting a textbook.

## Tone

- Precise. Every sentence carries a piece of the explanation.
- Elegant. No clutter, no apologies, no hedging.
- Curious. The narrator is interested in the object being explained, not bored by it.
- Calm. No urgency, no marketing.
- Adult. The viewer is treated as intelligent.

## Voice

Default to a measured, slightly warm professorial register. The narrator is someone who finds the topic genuinely beautiful and is sharing it because it deserves to be shared. Not a hype voice. Not a lecture voice. Closer to the register of an essay read aloud than to a tutorial.

## Sentence rhythm

- Vary length. Short sentences land definitions and conclusions. Longer sentences move through reasoning. Avoid uniform rhythm — it puts the viewer to sleep.
- Place the load at the end. The most important word in a sentence should usually be near its end, not the start.
- Use pauses. A 1–2 s pause after a definition lets the viewer absorb it. Mark pauses in the production notes column, not in the voice-over text.

## What to avoid

Banned phrasings:

- "this is a very important concept"
- "let's dive in"
- "in this video, we will learn about…"
- "as you can see…"
- "stay tuned"
- "buckle up"
- "get ready to have your mind blown"
- "without further ado"
- "the bottom line is…"
- "it turns out that…" (when used as filler, not as genuine surprise)
- "interestingly," "notably," "importantly" (as opening interjections)
- "pretty much", "basically", "essentially", "kind of", "sort of" (epistemic filler)
- "we" used to mean "I'm narrating" rather than "you and I together exploring" — keep "we" only when it genuinely models a joint act of thinking

## What to use

- The second person, sparingly: "Notice that…", "Suppose…", "Watch what happens when…"
- The first-person plural for genuine joint reasoning: "We can ask whether…", "We have two options…"
- Direct statement: "The integral converges." Not "what we can see is that the integral converges."
- Naming acts of thought: "Now consider", "Suppose for a moment", "Hold this image in mind"

## Definitions and theorems in narration

When a definition appears on screen, the narration does not have to read the formula. It can name the conceptual content:

> Visual: $\langle f, g \rangle = \int_a^b f(x) \, g(x) \, w(x) \, dx$
> Bad: "We define the inner product as the integral from a to b of f of x times g of x times w of x dx."
> Good: "This is the inner product the construction needs: a weighted overlap between two functions on the interval."

The viewer can read the formula from the screen. The narration adds *what the formula means*.

## Transitions

End each scene with a sentence that *opens* the next scene rather than closes the current one.

> Bad ending of scene 4: "And that's the definition of a manifold."
> Good ending of scene 4: "But the definition raises a new question — how do two coordinate descriptions of the same region agree where they overlap?"

The next scene then answers the question physically and verbally.

## Synchronization

The voice-over is locked to the visual. If a sentence describes an event, the event must be on screen during that sentence — not before, not after.

When a visual takes longer than the narration that introduces it, the narration stops and the visual continues silently. State this in the production notes.

When narration runs longer than its supporting visual, either lengthen the visual or split the narration. Do not narrate a black screen.

## Handling uncertainty

Where the script makes an informal claim that will be tightened later, the narration can flag it without weakening:

- "For now, think of $X$ as ..." (signals informal mode without apology)
- "We will make this precise shortly" (signals deferred rigor)
- "This is the picture; the formal definition follows" (clean handoff)

Never say "I think", "kind of", "sort of", "basically" — these undermine the narrator's authority.

## Closing scenes

The video ends on a *re-encounter* with the opening question, not on a definition. The closing narration should make the viewer feel that something has shifted.

> Opening: "What does it mean for a curved object to be locally coordinate-like?"
> Closing: "Now the question has a precise answer — and the same construction underlies every smooth space we will meet from here."

## Reading-aloud test

Read the voice-over-only block aloud. If a sentence makes you stumble, rephrase. If a paragraph reads like a textbook, rewrite. The voice-over should be speakable.

For TTS systems: avoid em-dashes followed by close-quote (TTS often misreads), avoid stacked subordinate clauses, prefer punctuation that matches breath. Spell out symbols TTS will mispronounce ("phi" not "ϕ").
