# Core claim

An experimental demonstration that *taking a memory test* on studied material improves long-term retention more than restudying the same material for an equivalent time, even when no feedback is given. The effect grows with retention interval: one week later, the testing condition shows ~50% better recall than the rereading condition.

# What this paper is useful for in educational video design

The clearest single-experiment justification for the *testing effect* (a.k.a. retrieval practice). It is the empirical anchor behind every recommendation to insert retrieval prompts into educational content.

# Actionable principles

1. A 5-second pause where the viewer is asked to predict, recall, or classify is more valuable than the same 5 seconds of additional re-exposure.
2. Even *unsuccessful* retrieval attempts benefit later learning when feedback follows.
3. Effects grow over delay: short post-tests will *understate* the long-term benefit of retrieval prompts in a video — choose evaluation accordingly when assessing video effectiveness.

# How the video-script skill should use this

Insert a retrieval prompt at the boundary of every major scene ("Pause and try to predict X", "Which of these is *not* a chart?"), with the answer revealed visually a beat later. Include such prompts even in fast-paced explainers — a 3-second silent beat is enough.

# Risks / limitations / caveats

The studies use prose; symbolic / mathematical materials show analogous but smaller effects. Without feedback, the benefit accrues only to items that were *retrievable*; pure failure without feedback adds little. In a video, this means: when posing a retrieval prompt, ensure the answer follows within a few seconds.
