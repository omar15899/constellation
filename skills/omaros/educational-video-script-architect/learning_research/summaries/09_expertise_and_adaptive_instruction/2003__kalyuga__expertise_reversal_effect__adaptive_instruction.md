# Core claim

The **expertise reversal effect**: instructional designs that benefit novices (worked examples, redundant explanations, scaffolding) often *hurt* learners with prior knowledge, because the redundant scaffolding becomes extraneous load. As expertise grows, scaffolding should be faded.

# What this paper is useful for in educational video design

The single most important paper for adapting video design to audience level. Justifies the audience-tier table in `SKILL.md` §4: a beginner video should not look like a research-level video and vice versa.

# Actionable principles

1. **Beginner / undergraduate** videos: use full worked examples, generous signalling, slow pacing, redundant verbal+visual cues.
2. **Advanced / research** videos: drop the scaffolding. Definitions can lead. Examples can be motivating instances rather than solved templates. Pacing can be brisk.
3. Mixed-audience videos must choose: bridge with one concrete instance, then lift; do not try to satisfy both tiers simultaneously, or the video will under-serve both.

# How the video-script skill should use this

The audience-level decision in Phase A of the workflow controls every subsequent decision. The skill should never produce one design and *then* attempt to adapt it — adaptation must be at the design phase.

# Risks / limitations / caveats

The reversal is robust but graded — most learners are somewhere on the novice-expert continuum. When in doubt, lean novice; the worst-case overshoot is mild boredom for experts.
