# Core claim

A *Nature Reviews Psychology* synthesis arguing that **spacing** and **retrieval practice** are the two most robust, generalisable, and transfer-friendly learning interventions in cognitive science. Both produce *desirable difficulty* — short-term cost, long-term durability — and both work additively when combined.

# What this paper is useful for in educational video design

The most up-to-date single-source justification for inserting retrieval prompts and for spacing concepts across a video, a series, or a curriculum. Stronger and more recent than Dunlosky et al. (2013) on these two specific principles.

# Actionable principles

1. **Retrieval before re-explanation.** When a concept reappears in a later scene, ask the viewer to recall what was established before showing it again.
2. **Successive relearning** (retrieve → restudy → retrieve again) is more effective than one-shot exposure.
3. **Spacing is dose-dependent**: bigger gaps help long-term retention but cost short-term performance — be honest about the trade-off in series-level design.

# How the video-script skill should use this

When designing scene transitions, prefer formats that prompt retrieval over formats that re-show. When designing a *series* of videos, recommend spaced revisiting of earlier concepts in later episodes. When the user asks for a single dense explainer, the single-video version of the principle is to space *within* the video — re-encounter the core object after introducing intermediate examples.

# Risks / limitations / caveats

Most lab evidence is verbal; transfer to symbolic mathematical material is plausible and consistent with the broader literature, but with thinner direct evidence. Spacing benefits depend on the test delay — a short final test can mask the long-term benefit and even penalise spacing.
