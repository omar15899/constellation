# Core claim

A *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* monograph that ranks ten common study techniques by the strength of evidence supporting them. **Practice testing** (retrieval practice) and **distributed practice** (spacing) are rated *high utility*; elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, and interleaved practice are rated *moderate*; rereading, highlighting, summarisation, keyword mnemonic, and imagery for text are rated *low*.

# What this paper is useful for in educational video design

It is the canonical authority for the claim that *some* study techniques are robustly more effective than others, and that the techniques learners spontaneously use (rereading, highlighting) tend to be the *least* effective. Use it whenever the script needs to justify why a video should engineer retrieval moments rather than restate content.

# Actionable principles

1. Engineer at least one retrieval moment per major scene (not just exposure).
2. Distribute conceptually related content across scenes rather than packing it into one block.
3. Treat passive rereading-equivalents (a wall of equations, a long voice-over over a static slide) as low-utility and replace them with active formats.

# How the video-script skill should use this

Cite this when justifying retrieval-prompt insertions, spacing of related concepts across scenes, and the avoidance of passive exposure as the only learning mode. It is the highest-credibility single citation for the field.

# Risks / limitations / caveats

Reviews study techniques in classroom and self-study contexts, not specifically video. Inference to video pacing is plausible but not direct. The paper rates utility *across many conditions* — for a specific topic and audience, lower-rated techniques (e.g., self-explanation) may still be highly effective.
