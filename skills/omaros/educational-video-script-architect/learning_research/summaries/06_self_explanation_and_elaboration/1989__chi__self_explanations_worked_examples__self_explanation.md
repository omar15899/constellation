# Core claim

The origin paper for the **self-explanation effect**. Students who spontaneously generate explanations of worked-example steps to themselves learn substantially more than students who do not, controlling for time and content. The key behaviour is *generating* — not the explanation's accuracy.

# What this paper is useful for in educational video design

Establishes that *what the learner does inside their head* matters more than *what the video shows*. A retrieval prompt that asks the viewer to explain *why* a step works is more powerful than the same prompt asking *what* the next step is.

# Actionable principles

1. Pause after a non-obvious step and ask the viewer to explain it to themselves before revealing the explanation.
2. "Why" prompts outperform "what" prompts for deep learning.
3. Even silent self-explanation works; verbalisation is not required.

# How the video-script skill should use this

Insert at least one *why-prompt* per scene with a non-obvious move. The script's narration should briefly stop after the prompt to give the viewer time.

# Risks / limitations / caveats

Spontaneous self-explanation varies enormously across learners; novices may need explicit prompting. Self-explanation can produce wrong explanations — pair with a quick narration of the correct one to repair.

self-explanation effect; worked examples; generative learning; metacognition.
