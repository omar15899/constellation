# Core claim

Articulates the **region-of-proximal-learning model**: learners (when allowed to) tend to allocate study time to material at the edge of their current competence, neither too easy nor too hard. Metacognitive judgements (how well do I know this?) drive this allocation. The model also documents systematic biases — learners often *underestimate* what they have not yet learned and *overestimate* familiar-feeling but not-yet-mastered material.

# What this paper is useful for in educational video design

Explains why a viewer's subjective sense of "I understood" is a poor guide to actual learning. It justifies the script *engineering* moments where the viewer's overconfidence is exposed (a small prediction prompt that turns out harder than expected).

# Actionable principles

1. A retrieval prompt that the viewer fails — followed by the answer — is a calibration event. It corrects metacognitive overconfidence.
2. The video should resist the temptation to make every move feel obvious; *desirable difficulty* is a calibration tool too.
3. Naming a likely misconception explicitly before refuting it is a metacognitive move (Tippett's refutation framing, consistent with this view).

# How the video-script skill should use this

Pair retrieval prompts with calibration commentary ("if you are surprised — that is the point; the next scene shows why"). Avoid scripts that always confirm the viewer's intuition.

# Risks / limitations / caveats

Metacognitive judgements are noisy and individual. Engineered confusion can backfire if it persists too long; always pair with rapid resolution.
